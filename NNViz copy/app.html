From Polynomials to Deep Learning: The Mathematics of Influence

This document explains the mathematical journey from a simple algebra polynomial to a 15-layer Deep Neural Network, and how we can visualize the "influence" of individual parameters in both.

1. The Polynomial Influence Functions

Consider a standard quadratic equation: y = ax² + bx + c. If you want to know how much the final output (y) changes when you nudge one specific coefficient (like a), you use calculus. The partial derivative gives us the "Influence Function":

Influence of a = x²
Influence of b = x
Influence of c = 1

This means the coefficient a has zero influence at the center of the graph (x=0), but massive influence at the edges. The coefficient c has a constant, flat influence everywhere. If you draw these three influences as lines and add them together, you get the final parabola. This is called Additive Decomposition.

2. Translating to a Neural Network

A neural network is not magic; it is just a massive system of polynomial-like additions. Before a neuron applies its activation function, it calculates a value Z:

Z = (w₁ · A₁) + (w₂ · A₂) + (w₃ · A₃) + b

The "Aha!" Moment

Look at the equation above. It is structurally identical to y = ax² + bx + c.
  • w₁, w₂, w₃ are just like your coefficients a, b, c.
  • A₁, A₂, A₃ are the inputs from the previous layer (acting like the x², x, and 1).
  • b is exactly the same as the constant c in your polynomial.
Therefore, we can apply Additive Decomposition to a neuron! We can draw the exact mathematical impact of w₁, add it to the impact of w₂, and watch them stack to form the neuron's final state.

3. The 15-Layer Problem

If a network has 15 layers, why don't we just draw 15 lines? Because of Non-Linearity. In a polynomial, changing a doesn't change the shape of b's influence. But in a Neural Network, weights pass through activation functions (like ReLU or Sigmoid). This means the weights in Layer 1 are mathematically "tangled" with the weights in Layer 2.

You cannot draw a simple isolated line for a deep network. Instead, Deep Learning engineers visualize Gradient Flow—using the Chain Rule to measure how fast the "influence" fades as it moves backward through the 15 layers.
